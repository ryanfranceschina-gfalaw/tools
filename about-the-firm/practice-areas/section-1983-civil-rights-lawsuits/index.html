Section 1983 Civil Rights Lawsuits

When police officers, jailers, or other government officials violate your constitutional rights, federal law lets you hold them accountable. 42 U.S.C. § 1983 allows a person to sue anyone who, acting under color of state law, deprives them of rights secured by the Constitution or federal law. GFA LAW, PLLC files these lawsuits for clients in Temple, Bell County and across Texas.

Cases We Handle

  • Excessive force and unreasonable seizures
  • False arrest and malicious prosecution
  • Retaliation for recording police, protesting, or criticizing public officials
  • Unlawful searches of homes, vehicles and phones
  • Monell claims against cities and counties whose policies or customs caused the violation
  • Jail and detention conditions

Qualified Immunity

Officers sued under Section 1983 usually raise qualified immunity, which shields them unless they violated clearly established law. Getting past that defense takes careful pleading, early evidence work, and a working command of Fifth Circuit and Supreme Court precedent. Our attorneys litigate these fights in federal court.

Deadlines Matter

In Texas, most Section 1983 claims must be filed within two years of the violation, and some related claims and notice requirements run shorter. Evidence such as body-camera footage can also be lost if no one asks for it in time. If you believe your rights were violated, contact us promptly.

What to Gather

  • Dates, times, places, and the names or badge numbers of the officers involved
  • Any video, photos, or witness contact information
  • Citations, arrest paperwork, booking records and medical records

How a Section 1983 Case Works

  • Investigation. We gather the video, reports and records, often through requests under the Texas Public Information Act (Texas Government Code Chapter 552), and we identify every officer and agency involved.
  • Filing. Most Section 1983 cases are filed in federal district court. Texas has four federal districts, and Temple and Bell County are in the Western District of Texas.
  • Early motions. Defendants often move to dismiss and assert qualified immunity before discovery begins. How the complaint is written matters at this stage.
  • Discovery. If the case survives, both sides exchange documents, take depositions and retain experts.
  • Resolution. Cases can settle at any stage or go to trial before a jury. You decide whether to accept any settlement.

State Law Claims and Notice Requirements

Some cases also include claims under Texas law. The Texas Tort Claims Act generally requires written notice to a government unit within six months of the incident (Texas Civil Practice and Remedies Code Section 101.101), and some city charters set shorter deadlines. Missing a notice deadline can end a claim before it starts, which is one more reason to talk to a lawyer early.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I sue the city or county, not just the officer?

Yes, in some cases. A city or county is not automatically liable for its officers, but it can be held responsible when its own policy, custom, training failure or decision by a final policymaker caused the violation. These are called Monell claims.

I was charged with a crime. Can I still sue?

Possibly. If a civil rights claim would mean your conviction was invalid, the conviction usually has to be overturned or dismissed first. Pending criminal charges also affect timing and strategy, so we look at the criminal case and the civil case together.

Should I file a complaint with internal affairs first?

Talk to a lawyer before you give any statement. Internal affairs complaints can be useful, but what you say can be used in the criminal case or the lawsuit.

What does it cost?

Requesting a case review is free. If we can help, the consultation is $350. If we take your case, we explain the fee structure in writing before any work begins. Federal law also allows a court to order defendants to pay a prevailing plaintiff's attorney's fees.
